A little everyday nostalgia, ready to print

A milk float on the morning round, a record player in the sitting room, a thermos packed for a day at the seaside: this word search gathers familiar details of everyday British life. Download the free large-print A4 puzzle and its separate answer sheet below, with no sign-up or email request.

The 12 words evoke the everyday-life feel of the 1950s–1980s without asking you to match each object to a decade. Many existed outside those years, and some are still with us. This original grid was created for the free resource; it is not a page reproduced from the British Nostalgia book.

How the puzzle works

Find 12 words in a 15 × 15 grid. Words read left to right, top to bottom, or diagonally down to the right. There are no backwards or upward words. Spaces disappear in the grid: MILK FLOAT becomes MILKFLOAT. A letter can belong to more than one word.

Cross each word off as you find it. If you prefer a gentler start, search for the first letter of one word across a row, then check right, down and diagonally. For a shared activity, take turns choosing the next word rather than racing. Difficulty depends on the solver; there is no time limit.

Print it clearly on A4

Choose A4 portrait, actual size or 100%, and turn off additional browser headers and footers if you print from a browser. Use the PDF file rather than a screenshot. Print only the puzzle for the solver; keep the answer sheet separate. If your printer clips the page, check the paper setting before choosing “fit”. Scaling down also makes the letters smaller.

Grid letters are 20 point and the word list is 18 point at 100%. Black letters sit on white with generous margins. The answers include a highlighted grid plus start and end coordinates. Large type can be helpful, but no single layout suits everyone; check a printed copy with the person using it. This puzzle is a leisure activity and makes no medical or cognitive-benefit claims.

Use it as a conversation starter

After solving, choose one word and ask an open question: “Have you ever used a record player?” or “What would go in your thermos?” Let the person decide whether to talk. Nostalgia is personal, and not everyone has happy associations with the same things. Avoid treating the activity as a memory test.

For an activity group, give each person a clear copy, provide a comfortable place to sit and let people work alone or together. You can cover part of the list to focus on a few words, or use the answer coordinates to offer a clue without solving the whole puzzle.
