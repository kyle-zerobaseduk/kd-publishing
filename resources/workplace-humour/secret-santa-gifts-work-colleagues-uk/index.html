Funny Secret Santa Gifts for Work Colleagues: A UK Guide

Choose a thoughtful workplace gift using the agreed budget, how well you know the colleague and whether the joke will feel comfortable in the room.

By K.D.Publishing editorial · Editorially reviewed 9 October 2026

Choose the person before you choose the joke

A workplace gift needs to survive two moments: being opened in front of colleagues and being taken home afterwards. A joke about a shared situation can work; a joke at the recipient’s expense is much harder to judge. If you are unsure, choose something useful with a light touch of humour in the card.

This is a K.D.Publishing editorial guide, and we publish one of the books discussed below. It is not an independent ranking or a list of bestsellers. The budgets are planning examples, not verified prices or claims that particular products are available at those amounts.

Four questions to answer first

  • What is the agreed spending limit, and does it include delivery? Treat it as a ceiling, not an invitation to show up the rest of the group.
  • How well do you know the recipient’s tastes? Use something they have chosen to share, such as a hobby, rather than an assumption about their age or appearance.
  • Will presents be opened publicly? Imagine the recipient showing the gift to a manager or a new starter.
  • Are there useful restrictions: food allergies, dietary preferences, fragrance sensitivity, alcohol preference or workplace gifting policies? If unsure, choose a different category rather than guessing.

Work with the agreed budget

For an illustrative £5 limit, focus on one modest item or a thoughtful card with something small. For an illustrative £10–£15 limit, consider a puzzle or humour book, a useful stationery item, or a consumable you know they like. For an illustrative £20 limit, improve the fit or quality rather than assembling a bag of unrelated novelties. None of these examples guarantees a product will fit that budget: check the current total before buying.

Delivery and wrapping can absorb a surprising part of a small budget. A remote colleague’s gift also needs to arrive on time and travel well. Do not send fragile or perishable items without checking the delivery arrangements. If the only suitable item is over the agreed limit, find a simpler option. A gift exchange should not create a spending competition.

Five categories worth considering

A short humour book suits someone who likes recognisable observations and dipping into a few pages. A themed puzzle activity suits someone who enjoys that kind of pastime. Practical stationery works when the person already uses it; another novelty mug is less thoughtful if their desk is full of mugs. A food or drink treat works when you know their preferences and can check ingredients. A small hobby-related item works when you have a specific interest to go on.

Choose a gift that makes sense without a long explanation. “I thought you’d enjoy this on your tea break” is enough. If the explanation becomes “because everyone says you are…”, stop and reconsider. The gift should give the recipient something to enjoy rather than a label to defend.

Humorous, useful, or a bit of both?

A genuinely useful item with a mildly funny card can be a better choice than an object whose only purpose is one joke. For someone you know well, a humorous gift can feel personal without being intimate. For someone you hardly know, predictability is an advantage. There is no obligation to make the room laugh.

Use this test: does the joke target an everyday situation, or does it target the person? Meetings running long and rewriting an email are shared situations. Someone’s body, health, relationship, finances, religion or protected identity are poor subjects for a workplace gift. Avoid sexual gifts and messages about someone’s competence. A funny gift should not need the recipient to pretend they are comfortable.

Consider it for a colleague who enjoys recognisable office situations and short pieces to dip into, rather than someone expecting a notebook or practical email-writing advice.

A neutral note could read: “For all the replies that needed one more edit. Hope it gives you a laugh.” Keep private office disagreements out of the inscription.

Leaving gifts need a slightly different approach

For a departing colleague, the signed card may carry more meaning than a joke about escaping the office. Collect messages that acknowledge something specific they contributed, and keep the gift aligned with their plans if they have shared them. Do not assume leaving means retiring, being unhappy or starting a new role.

If a team collection is involved, agree who is buying and what the budget covers. A humour book can be part of a small leaving gift where the tone fits, but it should not replace a thoughtful thank-you. Avoid inscriptions that would embarrass the person if they showed the gift outside the team.

A quick check before you buy

  • The total fits the agreed limit, including any delivery costs.
  • The recipient has given you a reason to think they will use or enjoy it.
  • The message is comfortable to open in front of colleagues.
  • You have checked any relevant ingredients, delivery date and gifting policy.
  • There is no hidden subscription, unwanted obligation or personal dig.
  • If humour is uncertain, you have chosen the useful option.
