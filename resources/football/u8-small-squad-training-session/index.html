U8 Football Training Session for a Small Squad: A Practical 60-Minute Plan

Only five or six children at training? Use this 60-minute U8 plan for dribbling games and small-sided play, with an eight-player option and free A4 session card.

By K.D.Publishing editorial · Editorially reviewed 9 October 2026

The plan at a glance

Only five or six children have turned up, and the session you planned needs two full teams. You can still give them a useful hour of football. This U8 plan keeps everyone involved with a ball each, simple dribbling games and short matches that work without a full 5v5 game. With five players, use 2v2 and a rotating helper; with six, play 3v3. The set-up stays simple so you can spend your time helping children rather than moving cones.

This is an original suggested training plan for U8 children, usually aged seven. It assumes mixed confidence, five or six players, a safe outdoor area and a coach supported according to the club’s safeguarding arrangements. Eight-player alternatives are included. The hour includes explanations, drinks and reflection; it is not 60 minutes of continuous exercise. Shorten activities whenever the children need it.

Objectives and equipment

The aim is to help players keep the ball close, look for space and decide when to dribble or pass. Look for small successes: a child changes direction away from traffic, notices a free gate or helps a teammate. There is no required number of goals or technical score.

  • One size 3 football per child, plus two spares; 16 flat cones; bibs in two colours; a watch; water available at the sideline.
  • Use four two-cone goals rather than portable goals if secure goals are unavailable. Check the surface, run-off space and footwear before starting.
  • Start with an approximately 20 × 15 metre rectangle and six gates about one metre wide. These are suggested training dimensions, not official match-pitch requirements. Enlarge it if children repeatedly collide.
  • Have the club’s first-aid and emergency arrangements, attendance list and parent/carer contact procedure in place.

The 60-minute timetable

Each activity allowance includes its explanation and reset. Demonstrate once, let the children try, then offer one short coaching cue. Water is available throughout; the planned pauses are reminders, not restrictions.

0–8 minutes: arrive with a ball

Let each child dribble inside the rectangle as they arrive. Ask them to find an empty space, turn back into the area and stop the ball with a sole. After everyone is present, add gentle changes of speed and direction. Do not start with laps or a race that rewards only the quickest child.

Use a short phrase such as “little touches when it’s busy”. If a child is worried about joining in, let them explore one side of the area beside an adult before joining the group. For a more confident child, suggest trying the other foot; do not demand a particular move from everyone.

8–20 minutes: find a different gate

Spread six gates around the area with room between them. Children dribble through a gate, turn, then find another. There is no elimination and no goalkeeper. Start with free exploration, then ask players to see how many different gates they can visit in a short round. Reset personal counts rather than announcing a winner.

Try three rounds of about three minutes, using the remaining time for a demonstration and pauses. Progress by asking children to glance up before selecting a gate. Make it easier with wider gates and more space. If traffic builds up, move two gates to the quiet side rather than telling everyone to run faster.

Coaching cues: “Can you see a free gate?” and “How close does your ball need to stay?” Give children time to answer by playing. Avoid calling out instructions on every touch.

23–35 minutes: keep it or share it

With six players, make three pairs, one ball per pair. Each pair moves between gates. The player on the ball can dribble through a gate or pass through it for the partner to collect. Partners change who starts each short round. Show the receiver how to stand where they can see the ball, with space to move.

With five players, make one pair and one trio. In the trio, two children pass or dribble while the third offers another receiving option; rotate the starter every minute. With eight, make four pairs. This is an unopposed practice, so nobody waits in a queue or gets stranded as a permanent defender.

For the last four minutes, offer opposition only if the group is ready: six players use two 2v1 games, five use one 2v2 game with a helper on the team in possession, and eight use two 2v2 games. Rotate the defender or helper after each one-minute round. Keep the unopposed version if opposition makes play stop. Coaches should supervise both areas from a position where every child is visible.

38–55 minutes: play, pause, play again

Use four cone goals, two at each end. To score, dribble under control through either goal at the far end. Play short bouts of roughly three minutes, with brief recovery and role changes between them. The 17-minute block includes these pauses. Put spare balls by the coach and restart with a ball rolled along the ground.

Five players: 2v2 with the fifth child helping whichever team has the ball. The helper plays inside the area and changes every bout so everyone has a turn. Six players: 3v3. Eight players: two 2v2 areas with two goals in each; an assisting adult helps supervision. These games are training choices, not an attempt to stage an official U8 fixture. No heading practice is included.

Watch for one thing: does a player find space after passing or turning? Praise that action, then allow the game to continue. If one child dominates, give that child a new challenge such as helping a teammate find a goal; avoid removing their enjoyment through lots of artificial restrictions.

55–60 minutes: finish calmly

Slow the pace with gentle dribbling and comfortable walking. Ask “Where did you find space today?” Accept a demonstration or a few words. Tell the group one specific thing they did well. Collect balls and count children out using the club’s agreed handover process. Make one note for next week while the session is fresh.

Adapt the plan when numbers change

For five players, the pair-and-trio and rotating-helper arrangements keep the odd player involved. For six, three pairs and 3v3 need very little rearranging. For eight, use four pairs and two games; do not set up separate activities that one coach cannot safely supervise. If a child leaves, stop briefly, recount and switch to the appropriate version.

For U7, retain the ball games but use games of no more than 3v3. In England, U7’s entry match format changed to 3v3 from 2026/27; U8 remains 5v5. Do not carry U8 substitution advice into an official U7 event. Check the current format and local arrangements with the club.

Safety, workload and the official guidance

Check the weather, surface, equipment and each child’s needs before starting. Secure any portable goals using the venue’s approved method; do not let children climb on them. Keep water and rest available. Stop for pain, injury or distress and follow club first-aid procedures. Avoid fitness punishments, weighted training equipment and forced repeated sprints. This plan has not been field-tested with your group: adapt it after observing the children.

The FA’s current handbook lists size 3 balls for U7–U11, requires shin pads for Mini-Soccer and says heading should not be introduced in U5–U11 training. Our training recommendation is covered shin pads and surface-appropriate boots. The activity timings and area sizes above are editorial suggestions, not FA requirements or an endorsed session. The sources below apply to England; clubs elsewhere in the UK should check their national association.
